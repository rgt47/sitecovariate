\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Background.} Multicenter randomized trials recruit patients at
many sites, and site may then be a source of outcome variability.
Site may enter the analysis model as a fixed effect or a random
effect, or through a treatment-by-site interaction. For
cross-sectional outcomes this choice is well studied\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Kahan2013continuous}{1},\citeproc{ref-Kahan2014binary}{2}}. Regulatory guidance from
ICH E9, the FDA, and the EMA asks that stratification variables be
included in the analysis model.

\textbf{Methods.} We compared five linear mixed models by Monte Carlo
simulation. They were no site, fixed site, random site intercept,
random intercept with a random treatment-by-site slope, and a
fixed treatment-by-site interaction. The outcome was measured at
four visits. However, the treatment effect was constant over time
and there was no dropout. The analysis was therefore equivalent to
one of subject means. Seven scenarios varied site size, the
presence of treatment-by-site heterogeneity, and a null effect.
Treatment was assigned by alternation within site, except in one
sparse-site scenario with permuted blocks. Each scenario used 1,000
replicates (2,000 under the null). We report bias, standard
errors, power, type I error, and coverage, each with its Monte
Carlo SE.

\textbf{Results.} Fixed and random site intercepts gave the same
results within Monte Carlo error in every scenario. The
interaction term did not reduce power. Without heterogeneity, the
model without site had a model-based SE about 20 percent too large.
Its type I error was 0.018. The fixed, random-intercept, and
interaction models had type I error of about 0.05. With
treatment-by-site heterogeneity, the fixed, random-intercept, and
interaction models under-covered the marginal treatment effect.
They covered the effect averaged over the enrolled sites. The
random-slope model covered the marginal effect, at some cost in
power.

\textbf{Conclusions.} Site should be included in the analysis if
allocation is balanced within site. Under near-balanced allocation
the choice between fixed and random intercepts matters little. The
treatment effect may vary by site. In that case the estimand should
be stated first, and a random-slope model suits the marginal effect.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

We begin with a practical question in the analysis of data from
multi-site randomized clinical trials. Should site (or center) be
included as a covariate in the analysis model, and if so, how?
The answer has consequences in practice. Two choices are
involved: whether to adjust for site, and whether to treat it as
a fixed or random effect. These choices can influence estimates
of treatment effect, standard errors, confidence interval width,
and statistical power\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Kahan2013continuous}{1},\citeproc{ref-Localio2001adjustments}{3}}.

Multicenter trials are conducted to increase enrollment rates
and improve the generalizability of findings across clinical
settings. However, multiple sites add a source of variability
that complicates the analysis. Patients within a site tend to be
more similar to one another than to patients at other sites. This
similarity tends to follow from shared clinical practices, patient
populations, and local standards of care. This within-site
correlation, or clustering, violates the independence assumption
of many standard statistical methods\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Localio2001adjustments}{3}}.

\subsection{Regulatory context}\label{regulatory-context}

Both international and national regulatory bodies have addressed
the role of covariates, including site, in the analysis of
clinical trials. The ICH E9 guideline states that `factors on
which randomisation has been stratified should be accounted for
later in the analysis'\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ICHE9guideline}{4}}. When randomization is
stratified by site, this implies that site should appear in the
analysis model. The United States Food and Drug Administration
(FDA) 2023 guidance on covariate adjustment reinforces this
position. It recommends that stratification variables be included
in the primary analysis model\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-FDA2023covariate}{5}}. The European
Medicines Agency (EMA) similarly advises that stratification
factors be incorporated, unless stratification was performed for
purely administrative reasons\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-EMA2015covariate}{6}}.

\subsection{The case for adjusting for site}\label{the-case-for-adjusting-for-site}

The main statistical argument for including site in the analysis
model is that it can reduce residual variance. This, in turn,
increases the precision of treatment effect estimates and improves
power\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Kahan2014rewards}{7}}. Outcome rates or levels may differ
substantially across sites. The differences may come from patient
populations, diagnostic practices, or supportive care. If the
analysis ignores this heterogeneity, the variance stays
unexplained in the error term. Adjustment for site absorbs this
between-site variability. It can therefore yield narrower
confidence intervals for the treatment effect\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Kahan2013continuous}{1}}. When allocation is balanced within site,
however, the main gain comes from a correct standard error rather
than a more precise estimate. We take this up in the next paragraph
and in the Discussion.

A further argument concerns the validity of inference when
randomization has been stratified by site.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-kahanImproperAnalysisTrials2012}{8}} showed that stratified
randomization induces correlation between treatment groups within
strata. If the analysis ignores this structure, standard errors
are biased upward, confidence intervals are too wide, and power
is reduced.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-linschotenIncorrectlyAnalysingStratified2022}{9}} provided
a clinical example in which failure to adjust for stratification
factors led to a false negative conclusion.

Site adjustment is a special case of the broader, well-established
literature on covariate adjustment for precision in randomized
trials. It differs from that literature in one respect relevant
to design. Site is typically a stratification factor set by the
allocation scheme itself\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Pocock1975allocation}{10}}. A prognostic
baseline covariate, by contrast, is chosen for its association
with outcome.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-wangAnalysisCovarianceRandomized2019}{11}} make the
general case for adjusting for prognostic baseline covariates,
including via ANCOVA, on model-robust grounds. The classical
trials-design literature treats the topic at length\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Fleiss1986design}{12}}.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-wangUtilitiesPitfallsStratified2022}{13}} review
the utilities and pitfalls of stratified analysis more generally.
This paper addresses a narrower, site-specific question. That is,
how should site enter the model (fixed, random, or interaction)?
We regard the more general question of whether to adjust for a
prognostic baseline covariate as outside its scope.

\subsection{Fixed versus random effects}\label{fixed-versus-random-effects}

A central debate concerns whether site should enter the model as
a fixed or random effect. Fixed effects treat each site as a
separate parameter to be estimated. On the plus side, this
approach makes no assumptions about the distribution of site
effects. On the minus side, it uses degrees of freedom in
proportion to the number of sites. Random effects assume that
site effects are drawn from a normal distribution. Only the
variance of that distribution is estimated, so fewer parameters
are required\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Falissard1996center}{14}}.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Kahan2013continuous}{1}} conducted a simulation study comparing fixed
and random center effects for continuous outcomes across 378
scenarios. Random effects performed as well as or better than
fixed effects in all scenarios. The advantages were notable when
the number of patients per site was small (10 or fewer). They
were also notable when treatment arms were imbalanced within
sites.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Kahan2014binary}{2}} extended these findings to binary
outcomes, comparing fixed effects, random effects, generalized
estimating equations (GEEs), and Mantel-Haenszel methods.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Chu2011continuous}{15}} also used simulation to compare methods for a
continuous outcome in multicenter trials.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Pickering2007smallcentres}{16}}
considered continuous outcomes when centers are small. We note that
within-site imbalance between arms is one of the conditions under
which these comparisons separate fixed from random effects. This
point matters for the design of our own simulation (see Methods).

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Kahan2015fewevents}{17}} show the practical relevance of this
comparison. They reviewed 206 multicenter trials in four major
medical journals. The median number of events per
center-treatment arm combination was just 3. In 50\% of trials
there were fewer than 3 events per center-treatment combination.
Fixed-effects models need substantial data per site to perform
well. In the majority of real trials this condition is not met\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-kimAnalysisMulticenterClinical2020}{18}}.

\subsection{Treatment-by-site interaction}\label{treatment-by-site-interaction}

If site effects on outcome vary, a natural question follows:
does the treatment effect itself vary across sites? That is, does
a treatment-by-site interaction exist? In a qualitative
interaction, the direction of the treatment effect reverses at
some sites. Such an interaction has major clinical implications.
It suggests that the treatment may be harmful for some patient
populations\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-GailSimon1985qualitative}{19}}. However, tests for
treatment-by-site interaction tend to have low power. The reason
is that individual sites rarely enroll enough patients to estimate
site-specific treatment effects precisely.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-edgarIncludingRandomCentre2021}{20}}, working with a binary outcome,
stress the distinction between variation in outcome across centers
and variation in the treatment effect. The second kind of variation
also bears on the estimand. With a random treatment-by-site effect,
the target may be the effect averaged over a population of sites
(marginal), or the effect averaged over the sites actually enrolled
(conditional). The ICH E9(R1) addendum asks that this choice be made
explicit\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ICHE9R1addendum}{21}}.

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Agresti2000strategies}{22}} discussed strategies for handling
treatment-by-center interaction with binary outcomes. They noted
that random effects models smooth extreme site-specific
estimates. When some sites contribute sparse data, these models
provide more stable inference than fixed effects.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Islam2024centerlevel}{23}} reviewed recent practice. They confirmed
that the choice of method for handling center effects can
materially affect trial conclusions.

\subsection{Heterogeneous site sizes}\label{heterogeneous-site-sizes}

Real multicenter trials rarely have equal enrollment across
sites. Some sites may contribute hundreds of patients while
others contribute fewer than ten. This heterogeneity in site
size interacts with the choice of analytic method.
Fixed-effects models can lead to patient exclusion when a site
has observations in only one treatment arm. This is common with
small sites. Random-effects models, on the other hand,
borrow strength across sites and avoid this problem\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Kahan2015fewevents}{17},\citeproc{ref-kimAnalysisMulticenterClinical2020}{18}}.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Localio2001adjustments}{3}} provided an overview of the analytic
challenges introduced by clustering, confounding, and effect
modification in multicenter studies. That review emphasized that
the correlation of outcomes within sites must be addressed to
obtain valid inference.

\subsection{Present study}\label{present-study}

Despite the extensive methodological literature, uncertainty
persists about when and how to adjust for site in practice. It
appears that the existing simulation studies have focused
primarily on cross-sectional outcomes. In many clinical trials,
however, outcomes are measured at several visits. We use Monte
Carlo simulation to compare five analysis strategies for such an
outcome in a multicenter trial. They are (1) ignoring site
entirely, (2) site as a fixed effect, (3) site as a random
intercept, (4) site as a random intercept with a random
treatment-by-site slope, and (5) a fixed site-by-treatment
interaction term.

We should state two limits of the design at the outset. First,
the repeated measures do not change the comparison. The treatment
effect is constant over time, the subject effect is a random
intercept, and there are no missing data. Under these conditions
the four-visit mixed model gives the same treatment estimate and
standard error as a regression on subject means. Our study is
therefore, in substance, a cross-sectional study with a reduced
residual variance. Second, in the main scenarios treatment is
assigned by alternation within site. This gives exact or nearly
exact balance within every site. That is, the design removes the
within-site imbalance under which fixed and random site effects
are known to differ. We add one scenario with permuted-block
randomization within site to examine this point directly.

We evaluate the five strategies under equal and unequal site sizes
of about 20 patients, with and without true treatment-by-site
heterogeneity. We also evaluate them under a null treatment
effect, and under a sparse-site regime of 4 to 8 patients per
site. We assess bias, empirical and model-based standard errors,
power or type I error, coverage, and the fit status of each model.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{ADEMP structure}\label{ademp-structure}

We follow Morris, White, and Crowther\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019using}{24}} and
report the simulation under the ADEMP framework.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Aims.} Compare five strategies for incorporating site in the
  analysis of a multicenter trial: no site adjustment, fixed site
  effects, random site intercepts, random site intercepts with a
  random treatment-by-site slope, and a fixed treatment-by-site
  interaction. The scenarios cross equal versus unequal site sizes,
  with and without true treatment-by-site heterogeneity. Further
  scenarios cover a null effect, sparse sites, and permuted-block
  allocation.
\item
  \textbf{Data-generating mechanism.} Detailed in the next subsection.
\item
  \textbf{Estimands.} The primary estimand is the marginal treatment
  effect \(\beta\). That is, the mean effect over a population of
  sites. When the treatment-by-site variance is non-zero, we also
  report a conditional estimand. This is the size-weighted average
  of the site-specific effects of the sites in the trial,
  \(\beta_{\mathrm{c}} = \beta + \sum_i w_i \gamma_i\), with \(w_i\)
  the proportion of subjects at site \(i\). It changes from one
  replicate to the next. The two estimands coincide when there is
  no treatment-by-site heterogeneity.
\item
  \textbf{Methods.} Five linear mixed models, fitted with
  \texttt{lme4::lmer}, that differ only in how site enters.
\item
  \textbf{Performance measures.} Bias, empirical SE, mean model-based
  SE, the ratio of mean model-based SE to empirical SE, power or
  type I error at \(\alpha = 0.05\), and coverage of nominal 95\%
  intervals. Coverage is scored against \(\beta\) and, in the
  heterogeneity scenarios, against \(\beta_{\mathrm{c}}\). Each
  measure is reported with its Monte Carlo SE (MCSE), following
  Morris Table 6. We also report the fit status of every model.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data generating model}\label{data-generating-model}

We simulate data from a two-arm parallel-group multicenter trial
with a continuous outcome measured at \(T = 4\) equally spaced time
points. In symbols, the model for the outcome \(Y_{ijk}\) of subject
\(j\) at site \(i\) and time \(k\) is:

\[Y_{ijk} = \alpha_i + (\beta + \gamma_i) \cdot \text{Trt}_{ij}
  + \tau \cdot t_k + u_j + \epsilon_{ijk}\]

where \(\alpha_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2_s)\) is the random site
intercept and \(\beta\) is the true treatment effect. The term
\(\gamma_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2_{s \times t})\) is the random
treatment-by-site interaction, and \(\tau = 0.05\) is a linear time
slope. Finally, \(u_j \sim N(0, 0.25)\) is a subject-level random
intercept, and \(\epsilon_{ijk} \sim N(0, \sigma^2_e)\) is residual
error.

We note that the treatment effect does not change over time, the
within-subject covariance is compound symmetric, and every subject
has all four visits. Under these conditions generalized least
squares for a between-subject contrast reduces to least squares on
subject means. As a check, we fitted the site-fixed model and a
linear regression of subject means on treatment and site to one
simulated dataset. The two gave the same treatment estimate and
standard error to seven significant digits. In substance, then,
each analysis is a cross-sectional analysis of subject means with
residual variance \(\sigma^2_u + \sigma^2_e / T\).

\subsection{Treatment allocation}\label{treatment-allocation}

In Scenarios 1 to 6, subjects are assigned to arms by alternation
within each site (control, treatment, control, \ldots). No random
numbers are used. A site with an even number of subjects is then
exactly balanced, and a site with an odd number differs by one
subject. As a result, site is orthogonal, or nearly so, to
treatment. We regard this as a limit of the design. It removes the
chance within-site imbalance that separates fixed from random site
effects in the cross-sectional literature\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Kahan2013continuous}{1}}.
Scenario 7 therefore uses permuted blocks of
size 4 within site, drawn afresh at each replicate. The block size
of 4 is an illustrative choice. With 4 to 8 subjects per site it
leaves both arms in every site, and it allows an imbalance of up to
two subjects in sites of size 5, 6, or 7.

\subsection{Simulation scenarios}\label{simulation-scenarios}

We examine four primary scenarios, defined by two factors.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Site size}: equal (20 patients per site) versus
    unequal (exponentially distributed with mean 20,
    minimum 4)
  \item \textbf{Treatment-by-site interaction}: absent
    ($\sigma_{s \times t} = 0$) versus present
    ($\sigma_{s \times t} = 0.15$, that is,
    $\sigma^2_{s \times t} = 0.0225$)
\end{itemize}

In all four primary scenarios, we set \(K = 20\) sites,
\(\beta = 0.30\), \(\sigma^2_s = 0.25\), and \(\sigma^2_e = 1.0\). For
the unequal-size condition, the vector of per-site sample sizes is
drawn once per scenario. It is then reused across all replicates of
that scenario, and it is not resampled at each replicate. We note
that `unequal site sizes' therefore describes one fixed, arbitrary
allocation of sizes. The realized sizes were
11, 4, 4, 4, 6, 7, 7, 50, 35, 4, 25, 15, 4, 15, 5, 55, 11, 35, 6, 15
in Scenario 2 and
14, 7, 57, 4, 14, 12, 7, 104, 16, 42, 38, 4, 16, 4, 12, 20, 27, 40, 5, 30
in Scenario 4. Each scenario draws its own sizes, so Scenarios 2
and 4 differ in total enrollment
(318 and
473 subjects) as well as in
heterogeneity. The comparison between them is therefore not a clean
contrast of one factor.

Three further scenarios use the same \(K\), \(\sigma^2_s\), and
\(\sigma^2_e\), with no treatment-by-site interaction. Scenario 5
sets \(\beta = 0\) with equal site sizes, to assess type I error.
Scenario 6 uses sparse sites of 4 to 8 patients, drawn once per
scenario as above (realized sizes
8, 7, 8, 7, 7, 5, 5, 4, 8, 4, 5, 7, 7, 8, 5, 8, 4, 6, 6, 7).
Scenario 7 uses permuted-block allocation, with its own draw of
sparse sizes (realized sizes
5, 5, 7, 6, 7, 6, 4, 5, 7, 8, 8, 6, 8, 5, 6, 4, 4, 7, 4, 6).
The sparse regime is the one in which the cross-sectional
literature reports the clearest advantage for random effects\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Kahan2013continuous}{1}}.

\subsection{Analysis models}\label{analysis-models}

For each simulated dataset, we fit five linear mixed models with
the \texttt{lme4} package\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-R-lme4}{25}}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No site}: \texttt{y \~{} trt + time + (1|subj)}
  \item \textbf{Site fixed}:
    \texttt{y \~{} trt + time + site + (1|subj)}
  \item \textbf{Site random}:
    \texttt{y \~{} trt + time + (1|site) + (1|subj)}
  \item \textbf{Site random slope}:
    \texttt{y \~{} trt + time + (1 + trt|site) + (1|subj)}
  \item \textbf{Site $\times$ treatment}:
    \texttt{y \~{} trt*site + time + (1|subj)}
\end{enumerate}

Models 1 to 4 report the coefficient of \texttt{trt}. Model 5 has
no single treatment coefficient. For it we report a weighted
average of the 20 site-specific treatment effects, with weights
equal to each site's share of all subjects (both arms). Its SE comes
from the model covariance matrix of the fixed effects. This
estimator targets the conditional estimand \(\beta_{\mathrm{c}}\).
Models 1 to 3 assume a common treatment effect, so none of them
allows for the variance of \(\gamma_i\). Under within-site balance
their point estimates are close to that of Model 5. Model 4 is the
only model with a term for the variance of \(\gamma_i\). It
therefore targets the marginal estimand \(\beta\).

Two-sided p-values use a \(t\) reference distribution with degrees of
freedom equal to the number of observations minus the number of
fixed effects. This count is in observations (about 1,600 in
Scenarios 1 to 4), not subjects. It is anti-conservative for a
between-subject contrast, and it makes the extra parameters of
Model 5 almost free. Kenward-Roger or Satterthwaite degrees of
freedom would be the standard alternative\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Kenward1997small}{26}}. We
have not used them here. Confidence intervals use the normal
quantile 1.96.

\subsection{Performance metrics}\label{performance-metrics}

Scenarios 1 to 4, 6, and 7 each use \(n_{\mathrm{sim}} = 1{,}000\)
replicates. Scenario 5 uses \(n_{\mathrm{sim}} = 2{,}000\) (two
independent runs of 1,000). For coverage near 0.95, 1,000
replicates give an MCSE of about 0.007. For type I error near 0.05,
2,000 replicates give an MCSE of about 0.005. For each model we
compute the following.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Bias}: mean estimated treatment effect minus the true value
  \(\beta\)
\item
  \textbf{Empirical SE}: standard deviation of estimates across
  replicates
\item
  \textbf{Mean model SE}: average of model-based standard errors
\item
  \textbf{SE ratio}: mean model SE divided by empirical SE; values
  above 1 mean the model overstates its uncertainty
\item
  \textbf{Power} (or, in the null-effect scenario, \textbf{type I error}):
  proportion of replicates with \(p < 0.05\)
\item
  \textbf{Coverage}: proportion of nominal 95\% confidence intervals
  containing \(\beta\); in the heterogeneity scenarios, also the
  proportion containing \(\beta_{\mathrm{c}}\)
\item
  \textbf{Fit status}: each fit is classed as converged, singular,
  nonconverged, or error. We note that \texttt{lme4} signals optimizer
  failure and singular fits as warnings rather than errors. The
  classification therefore captures \texttt{lme4} warnings via
  \texttt{withCallingHandlers()} and checks \texttt{lme4::isSingular()} and
  \texttt{fit@optinfo\$conv\$opt}. Every fit that returns an estimate
  (singular and nonconverged fits included) enters bias, SE, power,
  and coverage. Only fits that end in an error are excluded.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{Headline findings}\label{headline-findings}

We summarize the main results here; the tables that follow give
every measure with its MCSE. Absolute bias was at most
0.006 for every model in every scenario. The five models
differ in their standard errors, not in their point estimates.

First, the fixed-intercept, random-intercept, and fixed interaction
models gave almost the same results throughout. Across Scenarios 1
to 4, 6, and 7, the largest difference in power between the fixed
and random site models was 0.008. This holds in
Scenario 7, where permuted blocks allow within-site imbalance (power
0.626 fixed versus
0.633 random, MCSE about
0.015).
The interaction model's power minus the fixed model's power ranged
from -0.002 to 0.010.
That is, we see no loss of power from the interaction term. This is
in part a consequence of the observation-level degrees of freedom
(Methods).

Second, the no-site model overstates its own uncertainty when
there is no treatment-by-site heterogeneity. Its SE ratio ranged
from 1.172 to 1.226 in Scenarios
1, 2, and 5 to 7. Its empirical SE, however, was close to that of
the adjusted models. The loss of power from omitting site therefore
comes from an inflated model-based SE, not from less precise
estimates.

Third, with treatment-by-site heterogeneity (Scenarios 3 and 4) the
fixed, random-intercept, and interaction models under-cover the
marginal effect \(\beta\). They do cover the conditional effect
\(\beta_{\mathrm{c}}\) at close to the nominal rate. The random-slope
model covers \(\beta\) at close to the nominal rate. Its fits were
singular in 0.364 to 0.637 of
replicates, depending on the scenario. Details follow.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

The simulation results bear on several practical questions about
site in the analysis of multicenter trials. We shall take them up in
turn. We should keep in mind throughout that, in this design, the
four-visit analysis is equivalent to an analysis of subject means
(Methods). The results therefore speak to the cross-sectional
problem, with a reduced residual variance. They do not test any
feature that is specific to longitudinal data.

First, we found no difference between fixed and random site
intercepts that exceeds Monte Carlo error. This held for moderate
and sparse sites, and under both alternation and permuted blocks of
4. In Scenarios 1 to 6 this is the expected result. Allocation by
alternation makes site orthogonal, or nearly so, to treatment, and
then the choice of site model has little effect on the treatment
estimate. The design therefore cannot reproduce the advantage of
random effects reported by\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Kahan2013continuous}{1}}, which arises in
part from within-site imbalance. Scenario 7 adds modest chance
imbalance, and it too shows no difference. We do not regard this as
evidence against the cross-sectional findings. Simple randomization,
very small sites with a single arm, and fewer sites remain
untested. Our conclusion on fixed versus random intercepts is
therefore conditional on near-balanced allocation within site.

Second, the fixed treatment-by-site interaction did not reduce
power relative to the additive models in any scenario. We had
expected it to, since it adds 19 parameters. Two features of the
design explain the result. The p-values use degrees of freedom
counted in observations, so 19 extra parameters cost almost
nothing. In addition, under within-site balance the size-weighted
average of the site-specific effects is close to the additive
estimate. We have not studied the test of interaction itself (its
type I error and power). A recommendation to include the interaction
term only when heterogeneity is expected a priori\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-GailSimon1985qualitative}{19},\citeproc{ref-Agresti2000strategies}{22}} may still be
sound, but this simulation does not provide evidence for it.

Third, the benefit of adjusting for site came from the standard
error, not from the estimate. The empirical SE of the no-site
estimate was close to that of the adjusted models. In the scenarios
without heterogeneity, its model-based SE was about 20 percent too
large. This gave type I error well below 0.05, coverage above 0.95,
and lower power. With heterogeneity the pattern is less clear (SE
ratios of 1.094 and
0.976 in Scenarios 3 and 4). This is the
mechanism described by\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-kahanImproperAnalysisTrials2012}{8}} for
analyses that ignore a factor used to balance the allocation. In our
design, alternation within site acts as stratified allocation. The
result supports the regulatory advice that stratification factors
enter the analysis model\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ICHE9guideline}{4}--\citeproc{ref-EMA2015covariate}{6}}. It does so through valid inference rather than
through a reduction in the variance of the estimate.

Fourth, with treatment-by-site heterogeneity the choice of model is
also a choice of estimand. The fixed, random-intercept, and
interaction models target the effect averaged over the enrolled
sites. They cover that conditional effect at about the nominal rate,
but they under-cover the marginal effect over a population of
sites. The random-slope model targets the marginal effect and covers
it. It does so at a cost in power, and with singular fits in a large
share of replicates. The ICH E9(R1) addendum asks that the estimand
be stated before the analysis is chosen\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ICHE9R1addendum}{21}}. We would
add that the site model should then be chosen to match it. The
heterogeneity studied here is modest, with \(\sigma_{s \times t} =
0.15\) (a variance of 0.0225). Larger heterogeneity would widen the
gap between the two targets.

Fifth, the `unequal' site-size condition in Scenarios 2 and 4 uses
one arbitrary allocation of sizes, drawn once per scenario and
reported in Methods. It is a fixed design factor, analogous to a
fixed total sample size. The results may depend on that one draw.
Because each scenario draws its own sizes, Scenario 4 enrolled more
subjects than Scenario 2. This probably explains why its power is
not lower despite the added heterogeneity. Paired scenarios should share one size
vector in a future revision.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. Unfortunately, the
data-generating mechanism is simple. Outcomes are normal, the
treatment effect is constant over time, the within-subject
covariance is compound symmetric, and there is no dropout. As noted,
these choices make the repeated measures inert. A genuinely
longitudinal study would need a treatment-by-time estimand analyzed
by MMRM, site-by-time variation, or dropout. The number of sites is
fixed at 20. The p-values use observation-level degrees of freedom
rather than Kenward-Roger or Satterthwaite degrees of freedom\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Kenward1997small}{26}}. The results should be read as illustrating
general principles, not as guidance for a specific trial design.

\section{Future research}\label{future-research}

Several directions merit further investigation.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Non-normal outcomes.} Extending the simulation to binary
  and time-to-event endpoints would complement the present
  results for continuous outcomes. It would also address the
  findings of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Kahan2014binary}{2}} and\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-kimAnalysisMulticenterClinical2020}{18}} regarding sparse
  events per site.
\item
  \textbf{Complex covariance structures.} The present simulation
  uses compound symmetry for subjects, and only a random
  treatment-by-site slope beyond site intercepts. Examining unstructured or
  autoregressive within-subject correlations, and allowing
  random slopes at the site level, would better approximate
  real longitudinal trial data.
\item
  \textbf{Informative site sizes.} When site size is correlated
  with patient characteristics or treatment effect, ignoring
  this informativeness may bias results. Methods that model
  the relationship between site size and outcome merit
  attention.
\item
  \textbf{Adaptive designs.} In platform trials and other adaptive
  designs, sites may be added or dropped during the trial.
  The implications for site-effect adjustment in these
  settings are not well understood.
\item
  \textbf{Software tools.} R functions or Shiny applications could
  let trialists explore how different site-adjustment
  strategies affect their specific trial design. Such tools
  could support informed pre-specification of analysis plans.
\item
  \textbf{Bayesian approaches.} Bayesian hierarchical models offer
  a natural framework for modeling site effects and
  treatment-by-site heterogeneity. They have the additional
  advantage of incorporating prior information. Comparing
  frequentist and Bayesian approaches in the multicenter
  trial context would be useful.
\item
  \textbf{Empirical review.} A systematic review of how recently
  published trials in major medical journals (NEJM, JAMA,
  Lancet) handle site effects would quantify current
  practice. It would also identify gaps between
  methodological recommendations and applied work. This would
  extend the review by\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-Kahan2015fewevents}{17}}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Reproducibility}\label{reproducibility}

This manuscript was rendered with R version 4.6.2 (2026-10-29). The
simulation engine (\texttt{analysis/scripts/sim\_site\_covariate.R}) is a
plain serial \texttt{for} loop over replicates. It uses base R's \texttt{rnorm()}
and \texttt{sample()} for data generation and \texttt{lme4} (2.0.1), \texttt{dplyr} (1.2.1) for model
fitting and summarization. There is no parallel backend inside the
engine. Report generation additionally uses \texttt{kableExtra}, \texttt{ggplot2}, \texttt{knitr}, \texttt{rmarkdown}, \texttt{bookdown}. The
package versions above are those of the rendering session; the
simulation results were produced earlier by the driver and saved.
The project's \texttt{renv.lock} and \texttt{Dockerfile} pin R 4.6.0 for the
containerized build (\texttt{rocker/verse:4.6.0}). The R version used for
a host render may differ.

\textbf{Random-number generator.} Every scenario is run by
\texttt{analysis/scripts/run\_scenarios.R}, one R process per scenario,
with \texttt{Rscript\ analysis/scripts/run\_scenarios.R\ \textless{}id\textgreater{}}. The driver
sets \texttt{RNGkind("L\textquotesingle{}Ecuyer-CMRG")} and one master seed
(\texttt{sim\_seed} = \ensuremath{2.026\times 10^{7}}). Scenario \(k\) uses the \(k\)-th
independent substream, obtained by applying
\texttt{parallel::nextRNGStream()} \(k\) times to the state after
\texttt{set.seed(sim\_seed)}. The two halves of Scenario 5 use substreams
5 and 6; Scenarios 6 and 7 use substreams 7 and 8. Each result
therefore depends only on the master seed and its substream, and
the scenarios may be run in parallel. \texttt{sim\_site\_covariate()} does
not call \texttt{set.seed()}. Per-replicate \texttt{.Random.seed} snapshots are
stored as an attribute of each result, so any single replicate can
be regenerated.

\textbf{Data and code availability.} The engine is
\texttt{analysis/scripts/sim\_site\_covariate.R}, and the driver is
\texttt{analysis/scripts/run\_scenarios.R}. The saved results are
\texttt{analysis/data/derived\_data/sim\_\textless{}id\textgreater{}.rds}, one file per scenario.
The manuscript reads these files and runs no simulation itself. The
manuscript source, bibliography (\texttt{references.bib}), and CSL file
are in \texttt{analysis/report/}.

\section{ADEMP compliance statement}\label{ademp-compliance-statement}

We checked this simulation study against the reporting standards
of Morris, White, and Crowther\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019using}{24}}. The aims, data-
generating mechanism, estimands, methods, and performance measures
are stated in the Methods section. The RNG is seeded once per
scenario substream, as described under Reproducibility. Every
performance measure is reported with its Monte Carlo SE, and the
number of replicates is justified by a target MCSE. Fit status is
reported for every model and scenario. The main departure from a
full factorial design is that Scenarios 5 to 7 vary one factor
from a single reference condition.
